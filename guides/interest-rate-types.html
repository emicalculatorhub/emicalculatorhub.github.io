Fixed vs Floating Interest Rates

A fixed rate keeps your EMI steady, while a floating rate can rise or fall, and the right choice depends on how much change you can absorb. This guide explains fixed vs floating interest rates in plain language, shows the maths with a real worked example, and points out the mistakes people make most often. You can follow along with the loan emi calculator on this site, and by the end you will be able to check any result by hand and use the calculator with confidence.

How it works

There are two parts to fixed vs floating interest rates: getting the inputs right and reading the output honestly. The inputs are your responsibility, and they deserve a minute of care, because a calculator will happily produce a precise-looking answer from a careless guess. The output is the calculator's job, and it is worth reading the whole output, not just the biggest number. The extra figures, such as the total interest or the amount saved, often tell you more than the headline result.

A good way to build confidence is to try the calculation twice with slightly different numbers and watch how the answer moves. If a small change in one input causes a huge change in the answer, that input deserves extra care. If the answer barely moves, you can relax about it. This kind of sensitivity check takes a minute and turns a calculator from a black box into a tool you actually understand.

Worked example

Take a worked example. Borrow 1,500, at an annual rate of 9 percent for 180 months. The monthly rate is the annual rate divided by twelve, and the EMI formula gives a monthly payment of about 15,214. Over the full tenure you pay about 2,738,52 in total, of which about 1,238,52 is interest. Stretch the same loan to 240 months and the EMI falls to about 13,496, but the total interest rises to about 1,739,013, so the easier monthly payment costs roughly 500,494 more in interest. That trade-off is the single most important thing to understand before choosing a tenure, and it is why the calculator shows the total interest next to the EMI.

Common mistakes

Watch out for three traps. First, a guessed input is not a measured one, and the answer is only as honest as the guess. Second, a single result is a snapshot, not a trend, so one reading should never drive a big decision on its own. Third, averages hide variation, so a result that looks fine on average may still be wrong for your particular case.

  • Entering numbers in the wrong units or the wrong order.
  • Rounding in the middle of the calculation instead of at the end.
  • Trusting the first result without a rough check against common sense.

Practical tips

A few habits make the tool far more useful. Measure rather than guess, write the numbers down before typing them in, and run the calculation twice with the same inputs to catch slips. Keep a note of the result and the date so you can compare it next time, and re-check whenever something changes, such as a new rate or a different amount. Small, regular checks beat one big calculation every time.

Tip: change one input at a time and watch how the answer moves. That shows you which number matters most and where extra care pays off.

Fixed and floating rates side by side

A fixed rate stays the same for the agreed period, so the EMI does not change and budgeting is simple. A floating rate moves with the market, so the EMI or the tenure can rise or fall over the life of the loan. Fixed rates give certainty and usually cost a little more at the start, while floating rates can be cheaper when rates fall but need room in the budget if they rise.

The right choice depends on how steady your income is and how long the loan runs.

Frequently asked questions

Can I trust a single result?

A single result is a snapshot. It is useful, but big decisions deserve more than one reading: try different inputs, check the trend over time, and compare with a second source when the stakes are high.

What should I do if the answer looks wrong?

First re-read every input and check the units. Then try a rough estimate by hand. If the calculator still disagrees with your estimate, the input is almost always the problem, so correct it and calculate again.
